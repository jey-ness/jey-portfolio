I'm Jey, a product designer based in New York. I started in brand and web design, which probably explains why I still care so much about the feeling of things: the tone of a sentence, the logic of a flow, the rhythm of a page, and the tiny details that make a product feel calm and human.

Over the years I've worked across startups and scale-ups, building design systems, shaping product direction, and helping teams make sense of ambiguity. At Shoplift I built the design practice from the ground up. At Elementor I created web language and systems used at scale. At Clover, I'm working on product experiences that connect business needs, team workflows, and real user behavior.

I like being close to the messy middle of product work: research, roadmapping, workshops, writing things down, bringing engineers in early, and turning unclear problems into something people can actually use. The work I enjoy most is thoughtful, collaborative, and useful, with enough room left for delight.

Shoplift · Core Product
Test Reporting 2.0
UX ResearchData VisualizationContent DesignPrototypingUsability TestingCross-functional Collaboration

TL;DR

I redesigned the Test Reporting experience to reduce trial churn by helping users better understand and trust their A/B testing results. Setting up tests was easy, but many users ended them too early because of confusion, mistrust in metrics, or overwhelming data.

I focused on making insights more discoverable, improving usability, and communicating the statistical model's state in human-friendly language.

This was a cross-functional effort rooted in real user feedback, designed to build clarity, trust, and confidence into the product.

Impact measured through

  • Reduction in trial churn
  • Support ticket volume
  • Usability and satisfaction via CSAT scores and surveys

Release date

May 2025

Users

Trial users and new users

Team

CPO, product manager, statistician/data scientist, backend engineer, and full-stack engineer

Background

In Shoplift's early days as an A/B testing tool for e-commerce brands, our focus was on expanding testing capabilities to stay competitive, answering user questions like "Can I test X?" and driving growth. However, as we scaled, we noticed retention gaps: while users loved launching tests, they struggled to grasp key insights from their results. This meant some of our core value propositions were not landing, limiting our differentiation from competitors.

Over about eight months, through support tickets, user calls, and session recordings, we identified a major friction point: the Test Reporting screen. Setting up tests was seamless, but interpreting results over time was not. Users needed a clearer, more actionable reporting experience to turn their tests into decisions, and that is where we focused our efforts.

Discovery

The reporting experience is where users discover the value of Shoplift, and it sits at the center of the product. It contains sensitive financial information people use to make business decisions every day, so it needed to feel simple, clear, and actionable. As Shoplift expanded its testing capabilities and our users became more sophisticated, the reporting experience had to evolve too, without losing those core principles.

I broke down the lifecycle of a test by time and analyzed user sentiment for each period.

Learnings

Through user analysis during the test-running phase, we discovered that most friction occurred within the first 72 hours, especially for new users. Here's what we learned:

1. Users were ending tests too soon
  • Many users prematurely stopped tests due to uncertainty.
  • Some were not confident their test was set up correctly.
  • Small sample sizes led to exaggerated results: big green or red numbers and drastic lifts, positive or negative, made users anxious and prompted them to stop tests early.
  • Conflicting data added to the confusion. We ran two models: Bayesian, with rolling hourly updates, and Frequentist, updated daily. Some users saw a negative lift while the test was "trending positive," which created doubt.
2. Users did not trust that we calculated their data correctly
  • Some users noticed differences between our data and Shopify or GA4, which raised questions about how we calculated metrics.
  • Our metric definitions and calculation methods varied from Shopify and GA4, sometimes resulting in different conversion-rate figures.
  • Strong bot filtering led to variations in visitor counts, which required further explanation.
  • As a newer product, Shoplift was still establishing credibility, so helping users understand our approach became a key focus.
3. The reporting experience was overwhelming
  • The volume of numbers, charts, and tables caused cognitive overload, especially for first-time users.
  • Some framing felt scary or negative, such as "Inconclusive Data" or "Insufficient Data," leading users to second-guess their tests.
  • Managed accounts, with hands-on guidance, had a significantly smoother experience compared with self-serve users. Having someone explain the data, set expectations, and guide best practices helped users focus on what mattered most.
4. Improving reporting meant more than UI changes; it needed to add value
  • We were capturing valuable data but not surfacing it effectively.
  • We needed to future-proof the product. Multivariate testing, DOM testing, and custom goals were on the roadmap and would require deeper reporting changes.
  • Users frequently requested additional features such as goal progress tracking, time-based and segment filtering, and more granular breakdowns.
  • We had created high expectations with our advanced capabilities, yet we were missing some basic features that other A/B testing tools had built in.

These insights guided our redesign efforts, ensuring that improvements were not just cosmetic but strategically addressed user concerns and friction points.

Feedback loop

The insights we gathered shaped the scope of our focus, allowing me to define a clear design hypothesis and establish success metrics. With these foundations in place, I began working on wireframes and conceptual ideas, gathering iterative feedback from key stakeholders, including the engineering team, our statistician, the customer success (CS) team, and sales. Our discussions focused on several critical areas:

Statistical Integrity & Accessibility

Ensuring that our calculations were mathematically sound while presenting them in a way that was understandable and approachable for users. We aimed to balance scientific accuracy with clear, human-centered communication.

Technical Feasibility

Evaluating the development effort required, prioritizing efficiency by leveraging existing data rather than introducing entirely new backend structures. The goal was to minimize complexity while maximizing the value of surfaced insights.

Addressing Customer Confusion

Ensuring the design directly tackled the key pain points users faced in interpreting their test results. We worked closely with the CS team to understand recurring challenges and ensure the new design reduced reliance on support for self-service users.

Business & Growth Impact

Gathering input from leadership, including the CEO and sales team, to align the project with broader business objectives. This included assessing how the redesign would influence user activation, growth, and investor-relevant metrics like churn reduction.

Usability testing

As we prepared for significant changes, it was crucial to ensure that existing users felt supported and not disrupted. To achieve this, we tested a prototype with them, including some out-of-scope elements we were curious to explore.

Testing goals

  • Evaluate the new navigation: determine whether users could easily find what they were looking for.
  • Assess the perceived value of the new additions to reporting.
  • Gain deeper insights into how users analyze their test results.

Key takeaways

  • Positive-to-neutral reception: While some users appreciated the new look, others were indifferent and immediately focused on exploring the data.
  • Navigation clarity: Users had no difficulty finding relevant information. When asked to locate specific segment data, they correctly identified the corresponding tab on the first attempt.
  • Data transparency preference: Experienced users preferred seeing raw data up front rather than having it pre-interpreted. They valued our analysis as a secondary layer.
  • Visual variant views: Users actively engaged with image-based variant displays in the previous design. This feature was a key part of their workflow and needed to be preserved in the new iteration.

Prototype

View the prototype

Final designs

After incorporating the final refinements based on usability testing and adjusting for planned scope reductions, we arrived at the final experience for developer handoff. During this phase, we addressed a few remaining edge cases, including:

  • Ensuring compatibility with tests launched before the update.
  • Validating that updates to the statistical model did not retroactively alter results.
  • Accounting for all potential test states and scenarios to ensure a seamless experience.

Next steps

Following impact analysis and user feedback, we prioritized the remaining enhancements for future development:

Metrics/Goal tab

Introduce an advanced view so users can explore all test metrics together with day-by-day breakdowns.

Segment performance charts

Extend daily performance tracking to include segment-level insights over time, since users rely heavily on this view.

Expanded test timeline view

Show progress, milestones, trend shifts, and statistical significance in a clearer chronological story.

Upcoming feature integration

Represent multivariate testing and DOM testing cleanly inside the reporting framework as those capabilities launch.

Comprehensive overview charts

Bring back cross-platform overview charts so users and support teams can compare Shoplift data with tools like Shopify and GA4.

Expanded reporting capabilities

Add dimensions like landing pages, product performance, geo-based analysis, and custom segment insights.

Shoplift · Feature Release
URL Split Testing Beta Program
Beta DesignFeature StrategyUser ResearchCustomer FeedbackMonetization Discovery

TL;DR

URL split testing unlocked a major gap in Shoplift's product offering: the ability to test entirely different pages, not just template variations. It was a highly requested feature and also a major internal need for our customer success team, who were spending time guiding merchants through workarounds.

I designed the beta program from the ground up so we could validate the feature in real conditions, learn from actual merchant behavior, and shape a more confident public release.

Impact measured through

  • Adoption and beta activation rates
  • Support ticket volume and resolution time
  • Qualitative feedback about setup, clarity, and usefulness

Beta

September 2024

Release

December 2024

Users

E-commerce marketers, growth teams, and self-serve merchants running landing-page and product experiments

Background

Before URL split testing, Shoplift users could experiment within theme and template constraints, but they could not easily compare two entirely different URLs. That gap showed up repeatedly in feature requests, trial churn, and support conversations. It also created a lot of operational overhead for our CS team, who were manually helping users approximate workflows the product should have handled natively.

The beta was not just a launch tactic. It became a structured way to learn quickly, reduce risk, and bring the team closer to real use cases before general availability.

Discovery

I framed the beta around two questions: what would merchants actually use URL testing for, and where would the experience break down first? We tracked requests coming through support, talked with users directly, and paid close attention to setup friction and post-launch questions.

  • Early hypotheses centered around campaign and landing-page testing.
  • Real behavior showed even stronger demand for price testing and product-detail-page experiments.
  • Users also surfaced needs around audience segmentation, product-specific redirects, and more advanced merchandising workflows.

What we learned

1. Our initial assumptions were directionally right, but incomplete
  • Users absolutely wanted landing-page experimentation.
  • But some of the most urgent value came from pricing, offer, and product-page testing.
  • That shifted how we thought about future roadmap opportunities, especially around profit optimization.
2. The beta itself became a valuable product-development mechanism
  • We could validate feature-market fit before a broad release.
  • Support and product stayed tightly connected to real user questions.
  • The process became reusable for future launches, not just this one feature.
3. Some of the biggest opportunities sat one step beyond the initial release
  • Smarter handling of URLs with UTMs or strings could create smoother setup.
  • Single-product testing deserved a more tailored path.
  • Audience-aware upgrades and profit reporting opened up future monetization and research directions.

Outcome

The beta showed strong adoption, helped reduce friction for support, and gave us clarity that the feature solved a real problem. More importantly, it gave us a richer picture of what merchants were actually trying to optimize, which shaped how we thought about the next generation of experimentation tools inside Shoplift.

Shoplift · Core Flow
Test Creation Flow
Information ArchitectureInteraction DesignUsability TestingDesign SystemsProduct Strategy

TL;DR

Creating an A/B test is the core user journey in Shoplift, but the original wizard was built for a simpler product. As the platform expanded, the flow started to feel rigid, cognitively heavy, and difficult to scale.

I redesigned the creation experience to reduce friction for new users, support future testing modes, and make advanced experimentation feel approachable rather than overwhelming.

My role included

  • Planning and PRD collaboration with the CPO
  • User research with CS and direct interviews
  • Hands-on design, prototyping, testing, QA, and post-launch analysis

Release

September 2023

Users

CMOs, e-commerce managers, and store founders

Constraint

Designed during a React-to-Vue migration and rollout of a new design system

Background

The old test creation flow asked users to make one decision at a time in a narrow wizard pattern. That worked when Shoplift supported fewer experiment types, but it stopped scaling once the product introduced more testing options and more nuanced setup needs.

We needed a flow that could reduce cognitive load for beginners while creating a stronger foundation for future capabilities.

Research

I pulled from multiple inputs to understand where the flow was breaking down: Hotjar recordings, customer success conversations, user interviews, and UX and copy reviews. Across those sources, the same themes came up repeatedly.

  • Users did not always understand what kind of test to create.
  • The experience felt too linear for a more complex product.
  • New users needed guidance, while experienced users wanted to move faster.

Design goals

Primary goals

  • Reduce cognitive overload during setup
  • Shorten time to create a valid test
  • Help newer users get started through a test library
  • Make the architecture flexible enough for future experimentation modes

Process

I worked through wireframes, internal usability testing, and iterative refinement with engineering so we could improve the flow without destabilizing a critical part of the product. The redesign had to be clearer for users and also realistic to implement in phases.

Outcome

The new creation flow made the core product journey feel more intentional and future-ready. It gave new users a stronger starting point, gave the team more space to expand experimentation capabilities, and aligned the experience with the evolving product strategy.

Shoplift · Monetization
Pricing Model Update
Growth DesignMonetizationSystems ThinkingContent DesignCross-functional Planning

TL;DR

This project touched almost every part of the product. We moved from a simpler usage-based structure to a more flexible pricing model that introduced usage options, feature-based access, and annual plans.

The challenge was not just designing a pricing page. It was redesigning every place where pricing showed up so monetization could feel transparent, understandable, and emotionally manageable.

Scope included

  • Signup and onboarding
  • Upgrade and downgrade flows
  • Paywalls and usage moments
  • Billing and reporting surfaces
  • Support for legacy plans

Release window

March to June 2023

Users

Prospects, new users, and customers on legacy plans

Team

CPO, PM, CEO, marketing, and engineering

Background

Pricing changes are never just a business exercise. They change how users perceive value, fairness, and trust. In Shoplift's case, the new model affected almost every screen where a customer could discover, compare, or pay for value.

That meant the work had to be both strategic and deeply operational. We needed to align the business on what we were selling, then make every touchpoint communicate that clearly.

Challenges

1. Pricing was connected to nearly every core flow
  • The redesign touched sign-up, billing, reporting, usage tracking, paywalls, and account settings.
  • We could not treat any of these screens as isolated moments.
2. Monetization friction can quickly become churn
  • Upgrade prompts, overages, and feature gates can feel punitive if not framed carefully.
  • The product needed to communicate value before asking for commitment.
3. The system had to support both new and legacy realities
  • Existing customers had different expectations and plan structures.
  • We needed a clear internal map so development and QA could handle edge cases confidently.

Process

I worked with leadership and product partners to map the full scope in Figma and Asana, creating a shared source of truth for design, engineering, and QA. This helped us keep stakeholders aligned and made an otherwise sprawling change set far more manageable.

I also researched pricing psychology and paywall patterns to balance clarity with conversion, especially in moments where users would feel the change most sharply.

Outcome

The final system supported a more flexible business model without losing sight of user trust. It gave the team a cohesive framework for how pricing should appear across the product and made monetization moments feel more deliberate, understandable, and brand-consistent.

Elementor · Design System
Elementor Website Design System
Design SystemsDocumentationComponent DesignResponsive DesignWorkshop Facilitation

TL;DR

The Elementor website was growing quickly, with more contributors, more launch work, and more teams needing to build within the same brand system. Consistency was getting harder to maintain and repetitive page work was slowing everyone down.

I helped turn the website's visual language into a real design system so teams could move faster, stay aligned, and build with much more confidence.

My role

  • Documented the web language and foundational decisions
  • Built UI atoms and components
  • Made the system responsive and production-ready
  • Owned handoff and ran workshops so teams could keep using it

Context

Built for Elementor's marketing website and adjacent teams

Core problem

Too many contributors, not enough shared rules

Team

Head of Design: Ronit Cyjon
Lead: Erez Eden
Design: Yanki Bindman, Danielle Stelman, Alon Braier, Omri Avraham, Yael Zlotnik
Dev: Asaf Rotstain, Alan Kaler

Why we built it

The website had become a cross-functional product in its own right. Designers, developers, and PMMs were all contributing, and other teams needed guidance for landing pages, campaigns, and supporting materials. Without a shared system, consistency was fragile and production speed suffered.

The design system started as a passion project and quickly became infrastructure for how the team worked.

Foundations

We translated Elementor's brand language into a usable web system: colors, typography, layout logic, and content hierarchy. Typography was especially nuanced because the brand relied on multiple typefaces and headline styles, so the system had to define where each one belonged and how they worked together.

We also designed desktop-first, based on actual usage patterns, and documented responsive rules so the system could scale reliably across breakpoints.

Component thinking

To make the system genuinely useful, we standardized more than tokens. We defined repeatable content blocks, media ratios, headers, footers, carousels, tabs, showcase modules, and other reusable structures that turned page building from a bespoke exercise into a faster, more reliable workflow.

Outcome

The system reduced repetitive work, improved consistency across teams, and gave new contributors a clearer runway into the product web language. Just as importantly, it created shared understanding. By the time I left, the team was continuing to grow it through workshops and internal adoption beyond the original website team.

Elementor · Platform
Elementor Experts Platform
Marketplace UXResearchIAPrototypingTrust Design

TL;DR

The Experts platform had to serve two different audiences at once: people offering Elementor services and people trying to hire them. The beta product already existed, but it struggled with discovery, trust, and information architecture.

I redesigned the experience to make it easier to search, compare, and evaluate experts while preserving alignment with Elementor's existing brand.

My role

  • Competitive and platform research
  • User interviews and community listening
  • Information architecture and user flows
  • UX ideation, low-fi design, prototyping, and UI refinement

Format

UX course project in collaboration with Elementor

Users

Freelancers, agencies, and business owners hiring web help

Problem

The marketplace lacked clarity, trust signals, and strong navigation

Challenge

The existing platform was already live in beta, which meant the redesign had to start from real limitations rather than a blank slate. Navigation and search were weak, categories and filters were unclear, and expert profiles did not reflect the different roles and strengths of the people using the platform.

There was also a deeper trust issue: without stronger reviews, ratings, differentiation, and profile structure, it was hard for clients to feel confident hiring through the marketplace.

Research

I combined competitive analysis with interviews, casual conversations, and social feedback from the community. That helped clarify not just what users said they wanted, but what the platform needed to communicate in order to feel credible.

  • Search and filtering needed to be far more helpful and legible.
  • Profiles needed to reflect meaningful differences between creators, agencies, and specialists.
  • Trust had to be designed into the platform, not added as an afterthought.

Outcome

The project reframed the Experts platform as a trust-building experience, not just a directory. By improving information architecture, search, profile structure, and platform credibility, the redesign made the marketplace more useful for both sides of the interaction.

Elementor · Product Feature
Container Feature Playground
Product DesignInteractive LearningEducation UXExperimentationCross-functional Collaboration

TL;DR

Elementor's Flexbox Container was a foundational product shift. It changed how users built websites, which meant education was just as important as feature release. We created Elementor's first product playground to help users understand the new model through hands-on learning.

The challenge was teaching a different mental model without overwhelming people who were already comfortable with the old way of working.

Outcome highlights

  • First product playground Elementor shipped
  • 81% of users reached the final slide
  • Created a new format for product education inside the ecosystem

Audience

Existing Elementor users learning Containers for the first time

Constraint

Users had mixed familiarity with Flexbox and strong existing habits

Team

Lead: Erez Eden
PMM: Gabriella Laster, Asaf Shevach
Design: Danielle Stelman
Dev: Asaf Rotstain, Nofar Shlomo

First thoughts

We knew many users were unfamiliar with Flexbox, and many others were resistant to changing their workflow. That shaped the learning strategy immediately. We excluded brand-new users, began with simple shapes instead of full website components, and tried to keep instructions light enough that users could discover by doing.

Designing the learning flow

The playground needed to feel guided without becoming a tutorial wall. We built it around clear next and back navigation, instructions, hints, a final result preview, progress indicators, and contextual container guidance. The whole thing was built inside Elementor itself using the same primitives users were learning.

Challenges and tradeoffs

1. Simplicity won over feature completeness
  • Long-scroll lesson ideas became difficult to load and navigate, so we simplified the structure.
  • Hints became collapsible to reduce clutter and let users try first.
2. We prioritized understanding over flourish
  • GIF-heavy approaches were dropped because of loading and development cost.
  • Keeping the final-result image visible helped users understand what they were building toward.
3. Team trust mattered as much as interface choices
  • Because this format was new, we needed close collaboration across teams.
  • Small copy and layout changes had outsized effects on completion and clarity.

Elementor · Website
Elementor Website Redesign
Web DesignBrand SystemsResearchA/B TestingCross-functional Leadership

TL;DR

I helped redesign elementor.com during a major brand transition and a pivotal stage in the company's growth. The job was not simply to make the site look new. It had to express a changing brand, support business goals, serve a broad community, and become a more scalable platform for the teams building it.

The project later won the 2022 Israeli Brand Awards for Best Web User Experience.

My role included

  • Research into the existing site, competitors, and KPIs
  • UX hierarchy, page structure, and design-system thinking
  • UI design and brand expression for the new website
  • Onboarding teammates, supporting dev workflows, and partnering on roadmap decisions

Recognition

2022 Israeli Brand Awards · Best Web User Experience

Audience reality

Heavy desktop usage informed a desktop-first approach

Team

Head of Design: Ronit Cyjon
Lead: Erez Eden
PMM: Sarah Nielsen, Daniela Soibelman, Johnathan Maimon

Why redesign a site that already worked?

The old website was not failing in the obvious sense, but the company had outgrown it. The brand had changed, the business goals had changed, and the product needed a more expressive, modern, community-oriented home. The site architecture was also aging, and responsiveness and accessibility needed attention.

This made the redesign a strategic product problem as much as a visual one.

Guiding principles

1. Design for how people actually browse
  • Most users browsed and purchased on desktop, which shaped page structure and hierarchy.
  • We still needed a responsive system that translated well to smaller screens.
2. Build a website system, not a set of pages
  • The work fed directly into a broader web design system.
  • That gave the site a coherent language and made future page building much faster.
3. Keep the community visible in the experience
  • The redesign needed to feel bold and branded, but also open and creator-focused.
  • It had to reflect what Elementor meant to its ecosystem, not just what the company wanted to say about itself.

Outcome

The new site gave Elementor a stronger brand presence, clearer information hierarchy, and a more scalable foundation for future web work. It also helped define how the product web team collaborated across design, development, and product marketing during a time of rapid growth.
