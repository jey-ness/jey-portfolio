Shoplift · Core Product
Test Reporting 2.0
UX ResearchData VisualizationContent DesignPrototypingUsability TestingCross-functional Collaboration

TL;DR

I redesigned the Test Reporting experience to reduce trial churn by helping users better understand and trust their A/B testing results. Setting up tests was easy, but many users ended them too early because of confusion, mistrust in metrics, or overwhelming data.

I focused on making insights more discoverable, improving usability, and communicating the statistical model's state in human-friendly language.

This was a cross-functional effort rooted in real user feedback, designed to build clarity, trust, and confidence into the product.

Impact measured through

  • Reduction in trial churn
  • Support ticket volume
  • Usability and satisfaction via CSAT scores and surveys

Release date

May 2025

Users

Trial users and new users

Team

CPO, product manager, statistician/data scientist, backend engineer, and full-stack engineer

Background

In Shoplift's early days as an A/B testing tool for e-commerce brands, our focus was on expanding testing capabilities to stay competitive, answering user questions like "Can I test X?" and driving growth. However, as we scaled, we noticed retention gaps: while users loved launching tests, they struggled to grasp key insights from their results. This meant some of our core value propositions were not landing, limiting our differentiation from competitors.

Over about eight months, through support tickets, user calls, and session recordings, we identified a major friction point: the Test Reporting screen. Setting up tests was seamless, but interpreting results over time was not. Users needed a clearer, more actionable reporting experience to turn their tests into decisions, and that is where we focused our efforts.

Discovery

The reporting experience is where users discover the value of Shoplift, and it is the core of the product. The reporting screen contains sensitive financial information users need to base business decisions on every day. Our product goal was to keep it simple, clear, and actionable. But as the product grew, the product evolved, and so did our users. We needed to expand its capabilities while still maintaining our core values.

I broke down the lifecycle of a test by time and analyzed user sentiment for each period.

Learnings

Through user analysis during the test-running phase, we discovered that most friction occurred within the first 72 hours, especially for new users. Here's what we learned:

1. Users were ending tests too soon
  • Many users prematurely stopped tests due to uncertainty.
  • Some were not confident their test was set up correctly.
  • Small sample sizes led to exaggerated results: big green or red numbers and drastic lifts, positive or negative, made users anxious and prompted them to stop tests early.
  • Conflicting data added to the confusion. We ran two models: Bayesian, with rolling hourly updates, and Frequentist, updated daily. Some users saw a negative lift while the test was "trending positive," which created doubt.
2. Trust in data was an important consideration
  • Some users noticed differences between our data and Shopify or GA4, which raised questions about how we calculated metrics.
  • Our metric definitions and calculation methods varied from Shopify and GA4, sometimes resulting in different conversion-rate figures.
  • Strong bot filtering led to variations in visitor counts, which required further explanation.
  • As a newer product, Shoplift was still establishing credibility, so helping users understand our approach became a key focus.
3. The reporting experience was overwhelming
  • The volume of numbers, charts, and tables caused cognitive overload, especially for first-time users.
  • Some framing felt scary or negative, such as "Inconclusive Data" or "Insufficient Data," leading users to second-guess their tests.
  • Managed accounts, with hands-on guidance, had a significantly smoother experience compared with self-serve users. Having someone explain the data, set expectations, and guide best practices helped users focus on what mattered most.
4. Improving reporting meant more than UI changes; it needed to add value
  • We were capturing valuable data but not surfacing it effectively.
  • We needed to future-proof the product. Multivariate testing, DOM testing, and custom goals were on the roadmap and would require deeper reporting changes.
  • Users frequently requested additional features such as goal progress tracking, time-based and segment filtering, and more granular breakdowns.
  • We had created high expectations with our advanced capabilities, yet we were missing some basic features that other A/B testing tools had built in.

These insights guided our redesign efforts, ensuring that improvements were not just cosmetic but strategically addressed user concerns and friction points.

Feedback loop

The insights we gathered shaped the scope of our focus, allowing me to define a clear design hypothesis and establish success metrics. With these foundations in place, I began working on wireframes and conceptual ideas, gathering iterative feedback from key stakeholders, including engineering, our statistician, the customer success team, and sales. Our discussions focused on several critical areas:

  • Statistical integrity and accessibility: Ensuring that our calculations were mathematically sound while presenting them in a way users could understand. We aimed to balance scientific accuracy with clear, human-centered communication.
  • Technical feasibility: Evaluating development effort and prioritizing efficiency by leveraging existing data rather than introducing entirely new backend structures. The goal was to minimize complexity while maximizing the value of surfaced insights.
  • Addressing customer confusion: Ensuring the design directly tackled the key pain points users faced when interpreting test results. We worked closely with the customer success team to understand recurring challenges and reduce reliance on support for self-service users.
  • Business and growth impact: Gathering input from leadership, including the CEO and sales team, to align the project with broader business objectives. This included assessing how the redesign would influence user activation, growth, and investor-relevant metrics like churn reduction.

Usability testing

As we prepared for significant changes, it was crucial to ensure that existing users felt supported and not disrupted. To achieve this, we tested a prototype with them, including some out-of-scope elements we were curious to explore.

Testing goals

  • Evaluate the new navigation: determine whether users could easily find what they were looking for.
  • Assess the perceived value of the new additions to reporting.
  • Gain deeper insights into how users analyze their test results.

Key takeaways

  • Positive-to-neutral reception: While some users appreciated the new look, others were indifferent and immediately focused on exploring the data.
  • Navigation clarity: Users had no difficulty finding relevant information. When asked to locate specific segment data, they correctly identified the corresponding tab on the first attempt.
  • Data transparency preference: Experienced users preferred seeing raw data up front rather than having it pre-interpreted. They valued our analysis as a secondary layer.
  • Visual variant views: Users actively engaged with image-based variant displays in the previous design. This feature was a key part of their workflow and needed to be preserved in the new iteration.

Final designs

After incorporating the final refinements based on usability testing and adjusting for planned scope reductions, we arrived at the final experience for developer handoff. During this phase, we addressed a few remaining edge cases, including:

  • Ensuring compatibility with tests launched before the update.
  • Validating that updates to the statistical model did not retroactively alter results.
  • Accounting for all potential test states and scenarios to ensure a seamless experience.

Next steps

Following impact analysis and user feedback, we prioritized the remaining enhancements for future development:

  • Metrics/Goal tab: Introduce an advanced view that allows users to explore all test metrics simultaneously, with day-by-day breakdowns.
  • Segment performance charts: Users heavily rely on daily test performance tracking, so we aim to extend this view to include segment-level insights over time.
  • Expanded test timeline view: Enhance the timeline to display test progress, key milestones, and changes such as trend shifts and statistical significance achievements.
  • Upcoming feature integration: The introduction of multivariate testing and DOM testing will require dedicated representation within the reporting framework.
  • Comprehensive overview charts: Initially scrapped by the product team, but resurfaced as a critical need from the customer success team. This feature will help users and support teams cross-validate test data across multiple analytics platforms, such as Shopify and GA4.
  • Expanded reporting capabilities: Extend reporting to include additional dimensions such as landing pages, product performance, geo-based analysis, and custom segment insights.

Elementor · Design System
Elementor Website Design System
⚡

Why we built it

Many people were involved in the elementor.com project — new designers, developers, and product marketing managers joined constantly, making consistency harder to maintain. Other teams also wanted guidelines for landing pages, emails, and release material. We needed to spend less time on repetitive parts and more on complex layouts.

Fundamentals

Converting the brand language to a web language: primary brand colors plus sub-colors and greys. Three fonts became a design challenge — the two headline fonts (Ivar Text Bold, Oli Grotesk Bold) were divided into primary and secondary. Later we bought the full Ivar family for better web headlines. All hierarchies documented: H1–H6, body blocks, bullets.

~87% of users browse on desktop, so we worked desktop-first. Designed for 1920px wide screens with a narrow 1280px grid, later moving to 1440px as standard. Mobile breakpoints: 360px minimum, 1024px tablet at 768px grid.

Components

Limited image/video to 4 sizes: 3:4 in 2 sizes, 16:9, and 1:1. Paired with full text blocks (label, headline, body, bullets, button). Full page blocks cut page production from 2 days to a few hours. Added headers, footers, carousels, tabs, and showcase components.

My role

The DSM started as a passion project with Yanki Bindman. I documented the web language decisions, added UI atoms, then built out all remaining components and made them responsive. I owned the project from start to handoff. Just before leaving, I held a workshop so the team could keep growing it — which led to more workshops for the marketing and education teams.

Elementor · Product Feature
Container Feature Playground
🧩

Overview

The Flexbox Container was a long-anticipated Elementor feature that changed how people build websites. We created a product playground — a first for Elementor — to help users at all skill levels understand the new way of working. We consulted community leaders, the education team, and developers to invent the platform from scratch.

The challenge

Some users knew Flexbox, many didn't — and many weren't looking to change how they worked. We excluded brand-new users and started lessons with simple shapes before moving to real web components. Hints were hidden by default to encourage trying first.

What we learned

Less is more — small changes in copy and layout dramatically changed how fast users resolved lessons. Communication and trust within the team was the most crucial part.
